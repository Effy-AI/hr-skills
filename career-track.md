# Build a career track

**Career track builder** (`career_track_builder`)  
Interview you, then build one function's level ladder and competency matrix — including the anti-patterns and sideways moves most frameworks leave blank.

I want to build a career track for one function at my company — the level ladder, the
competencies that matter, and what each level expects. Interview me before you draft anything.

A career track is a grid: competencies down the side, levels across the top, and in each cell a
short description of what that competency looks like at that level. It exists so a manager, an
employee and HR can have the same conversation about the same standard.

Most career frameworks fail the same way. They're written once, in a burst, to look complete:
twelve competencies, six levels, seventy-two blocks of careful prose. Nobody reads them,
because nobody can hold seventy-two blocks in their head during a performance conversation.
**A framework's value is in being used, not in being finished.** Build it small, specific, and
grounded in the roles we actually have.

## Step 1 — Interview me first

Don't generate a framework from the role name. A track built from the word "Engineering" and
nothing else is generic by construction, and a generic framework is the one that gets
abandoned — it describes engineers in general, not the engineers here.

Ask me these in one short round, not one at a time:

- **Which function, and is this the individual-contributor path or the management one?** Two
  different tracks with two different ladders. Build one now, offer the other after.
- **What job titles do people in this function have today?** These are the levels, or close to
  them. A ladder invented independently of the titles on people's contracts is a second,
  competing hierarchy.
- **How many people are in this function?** Under about fifteen, four levels is usually right. A
  six-level ladder in a twelve-person team means most levels are empty.
- **Is anything written down already?** A spreadsheet, job descriptions, an old framework, a
  promotion doc. Working from what exists beats starting clean, and the output ends up in our
  own language.
- **What triggered this?** A promotion nobody could justify, a review cycle coming, an offer
  that got refused. The trigger tells you which part has to be sharp; the rest can be adequate.

Where my answers are thin, carry on with what you have and say which parts you're assuming.
Don't stall the whole track waiting on one answer.

## Step 2 — Build the ladder first, and keep it short

Levels before competencies, always. The ladder is what everything else gets written against.

**Four to six levels.** Fewer than four leaves no room for a promotion between joining and
senior. More than six and adjacent levels stop being distinguishable — if I can't say in one
sentence what separates level four from level five, no manager will be able to explain it to an
employee, and the ladder quietly becomes a pay-band chart with prose attached.

**Name levels with the titles we already use.** If people are called Engineer II, use Engineer II.
Renaming the ladder to something tidier means every conversation starts with a translation.

**One ladder per track.** IC and management levels are not one sequence — a Staff Engineer
and a Team Lead aren't ranked against each other, and putting them in one column order
asserts a comparison that doesn't exist.

**Check the ladder with me before writing a single competency.** Thirty seconds to correct now,
a rewrite of every cell later.

## Step 3 — Choose six to ten competencies

Every competency is a row that every manager reads for every report, every time. The cost is
linear in the count, and above ten rows people skim — which is worse than no framework,
because a skimmed framework still produces a confident-looking judgement nobody actually
made.

**Ten is a ceiling, not a target.** Six well-chosen rows beat twelve careful ones.

Most functions need roughly one from each of these, plus one or two specific to the craft:

- **Craft** — the actual discipline. Technical design, writing, sales method, whatever the job is
  made of.
- **Delivery** — turning work into shipped outcomes. Reliability, quality, follow-through.
- **Collaboration** — how they work with the people around them.
- **Influence** — how far their effect reaches beyond their own work. Usually this separates
  senior from junior more than raw skill does.
- **Values and behaviours** — how we expect everyone to act.

**That last one doesn't ladder, and shouldn't pretend to.** A Principal is not more honest than a
new joiner. Mark values and baseline behaviours as **flat** — one expectation that applies at
every level — and leave them out of the progression. Everything else defaults to **leveled**.
Choose flat deliberately, not to save writing.

## Step 4 — Decide what actually changes between levels

This is what separates a real ladder from an adjective scale. Before writing any cell, decide
which of these grows — usually all four, at different rates:

- **Scope** — how large a thing they own. A feature, a system, a domain, the platform.
- **Autonomy** — how much direction they need. Given tasks, given problems, given goals,
  setting the goals.
- **Ambiguity** — how well-defined the problem is when it reaches them. A spec, a bug report, a
  complaint, a hunch.
- **Influence** — how far their effect travels. Their own work, their team, adjacent teams, the
  company.

**A cell that differs from the one to its left only in adverbs is not a level up.** "Writes good
code" → "Writes very good code" → "Writes excellent code" can't be assessed, because nobody
can tell you where good ends. Every cell should move at least one of the four axes relative to its
neighbour, and the best ones say which.

## Step 5 — Write expectations two people could agree on

Each cell is one to three sentences of observable behaviour. Not a rating, not an adjective, not
a list of responsibilities.

**The test:** could two managers, looking at the same person's work, disagree about whether
this is met? If they could only disagree by being difficult, the cell is good. If they could
reasonably disagree, it's describing a feeling.

- Weak: *"Strong technical judgement."*
- Better: *"Reviews teammates' designs and catches problems before implementation, not just
  bugs after it."*

**The neighbour test.** Read a cell alongside the one to its left. If you can't say what changed,
one of the two is wrong — usually the higher one, written as "the same thing, but more".

Write in the second person, present tense — "You own", not "The engineer is responsible for".
The person being assessed is the one reading it.

**An empty cell is fine.** A track with four rows filled properly is usable on Monday. A track with
ten rows of prose nobody wrote carefully is a document people stop opening.

## Step 6 — Give each level its four extras

Cells describe the standard. These four describe the movement, and they live on the level:

- **Promotion signals** — what someone consistently doing this level's work looks like when
  they're ready for the next one. Concrete and present-tense, not aspirational.
- **Anti-patterns** — behaviours that look like seniority and aren't. Being the only person who
  can fix something. Reviewing everything. Volume without judgement. This is the most useful
  field in the framework and the one most often left blank.
- **Growth opportunities** — where someone at this level can go next. Up the ladder, and **at
  least somewhere sideways**: into management, into a sibling discipline, into a specialist
  branch. A ladder that only goes up describes a promotion path, not a career, and it's the
  most common complaint about frameworks like this. If a level is terminal, say so rather than
  leaving it blank.
- **Who belongs here** — prose describing who sits at this level: the job titles as people
  actually write them including variants, the scope and tenure signals, and the explicit
  exclusions. *"Senior Engineer, Engineer III, Lead Engineer with no reports. Not experienced
  hires in their first 90 days — level on capability, not time served."* The exclusions are what
  make it useful; a description with none is a job title in a longer form.

## Step 7 — Draft the edges, check with me, then fill the middle

Don't produce a complete framework in one pass and present it for approval.

Write the **lowest and highest level columns first**. They anchor the range, and they're the two
I'll have the strongest opinions about. Show me those two columns and the competency list, and
ask me two questions: *are these the right rows*, and *is this the right distance between the
bottom and the top*. Fix both before writing anything else.

Then fill the middle columns, where the answers now have something to interpolate between.

**The reason for the pause isn't politeness.** Handed forty blocks of plausible prose I didn't
write, I'll read the first three, approve the rest, and never use any of it — because it isn't mine.
A framework I corrected twice while it was being built is one I'll defend in a promotion
conversation.

**Everything must come from what I said or confirmed.** Where you're inferring rather than
reporting, say so in the moment — "I've assumed influence matters more than craft above level
four; tell me if that's backwards." Never present an invented detail as something we decided. A
fabricated expectation is worse than a missing one: the missing cell gets filled, and the
invented one gets used to make a decision about a person.

## Output

**The ladder** — levels in order, one line each.
**The competencies** — six to ten, each marked leveled or flat, one line each.
**The matrix** — the expectation at each competency × level.
**Per level** — promotion signals · anti-patterns · growth opportunities · who belongs here.

One to three sentences per cell. Mark anything assumed rather than confirmed, inline, so I can
correct it in one pass.

Close by naming what's still empty and what would most improve the track next — usually the
anti-patterns and the sideways growth opportunities.
