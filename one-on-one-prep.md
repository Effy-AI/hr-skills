# Prepare for a 1-on-1

**1-on-1 preparation** (`one_on_one_prep`)  
Produce a coaching agenda for an upcoming 1-on-1 — key themes, open action items, a conversation framework, and 4–6 questions tailored to the person.

Turn what the manager already knows about a person into a 30-minute conversation worth having.

A good 1-on-1 belongs to the employee, not the manager. Preparation exists to make the manager
useful in it — to arrive knowing what is unresolved, what this person is working on becoming, and
which few questions would actually move something. It does not exist to produce a script.

If the user hasn't said who the 1:1 is with, ask that first — one line.

## What this produces

A short brief, ordered from record to judgement: where things stand, progress on development
goals, what to close before the meeting, open action items, what's worth discussing, 4–6
questions, and feedback to give.

Only the first is always present. Every other section appears when there is something real to
put in it and is left out entirely when there isn't — a typical brief runs to four or five
sections, not seven.

## Step 1 — Find the history before asking for it

Almost everything a grounded brief needs is already written down in the manager's own systems.
Look there first. Asking a manager to retype what their notetaker recorded last Tuesday makes
preparation feel like more work than the meeting.

Where the history usually lives, in rough order of yield:

- **Effy AI, if connected** — look the person up in the employee directory (role, manager, start
  date); read the 1-on-1s, meetings and notes logged as activities between the two of them; their
  growth plan for the current period, with its status and history; where they stand on their
  career track and what the next level asks; and their latest performance-review reports, as
  far as the user's access allows. Growth plans and level expectations are the best source for
  *Progress on development goals*.
- **Meeting notes and recordings** — a notetaker or notes app holds the last few 1-on-1s almost
  verbatim. Find the last five. Often sufficient on its own.
- **Calendar** — confirms the cadence, when the last 1-on-1 actually happened, and whether recent
  ones were cancelled, which is a signal in itself.
- **Email and chat** — where commitments get made between meetings, and where a frustration
  usually surfaces before it reaches a 1-on-1.
- **Docs and drives** — running 1-on-1 pages, development plans, goal and review documents.

Search on the person's name and the obvious meeting titles. **Stop as soon as there is enough to
write a grounded brief** — a recent set of 1-on-1 notes normally settles it.

**Match the person, not the name.** A name search returns near-misses: a customer whose name
differs by a letter, a colleague who shares a first name, an external contact on a thread. Before
using anything, check it is genuinely a recurring conversation with this person — the right kind
of meeting, the right relationship, a plausible cadence. A prospect call read as a 1-on-1 produces
a brief that is fluent, fully sourced, and about the wrong person. Where the match is uncertain,
name the candidates and ask. Where nothing matches, say so and ask.

**Say what you're working from**: which sources, how many meetings, over what period. If you
found fewer than three past 1:1s, say the brief will be thinner.

Then ask only about what is still missing — once, in a single short question. If nothing turned
up at all, ask for the essentials: their role, when the last 1-on-1 was, and anything currently
open between them. Mark anything that comes from the manager's memory rather than a record —
memory is weaker evidence, and the manager should see which is which.

**Ground every statement.** Where context is thin, say so plainly — "no notes from the last
1-on-1 were found" — and let the section stand short. A brief that invents a prior meeting, a
commitment that was never made, or a goal nobody set is worse than a brief with gaps: the manager
walks in and references something that did not happen.

## Step 2 — Read the pattern, not the last meeting

Across several meetings, look for:

- **Open threads** — what was promised and never closed
- **Recurring themes** — the topic that keeps returning, which is usually the real one. If
  something has come up three times and hasn't moved, that's the meeting.
- **Development trajectory** — where they are visibly growing, and where the gap has not moved
- **Engagement signals** — energised, frustrated, uncertain, checked out. Quote what makes you
  say so.
- **Feedback history** — what was raised before, and whether it landed. Feedback given twice
  with no change is a different conversation from feedback given once.

**Compress all of it into three or four sentences.** This opens the brief and is read standing
up. Honest and specific: if the picture is mixed, say it's mixed. Don't round it up to
reassuring.

**Movement against goals they have actually set gets its own section** — advanced, stalled, or
untouched since last time, and which. Where no goal was ever set (no growth plan, nothing in the
notes), the section does not appear; don't turn a general impression into progress against a
goal nobody agreed.

## Step 3 — Separate what to do from what to discuss

A timed agenda mostly gets ignored: the meeting goes where the employee takes it, as it should.
Two things are more useful than a schedule.

**What to close before the meeting.** The manager's own outstanding commitments they can still
discharge in the ten minutes beforehand — a document promised, an answer owed, a decision someone
is waiting on. Walking in with your own loops open costs you the standing to ask about theirs.

**What's worth discussing.** Two to four things that would make this conversation matter — an
unresolved thread, a decision stuck on them, a pattern worth naming out loud. One line each on
why. Don't order them into a schedule; the employee's agenda comes first in the room.

## Step 4 — List open action items

Anything committed and not visibly closed, with who owns it and when it was first raised. Where
the record doesn't say whether something was finished, list it as unresolved rather than
guessing. Quote where you can: "you said you'd unblock the API access on 3 March" is useful;
"there were some blockers" is not.

Items the manager can discharge before the call belong in the previous section. Each item appears
once.

## Step 5 — Write 4–6 questions using GROW

- **Goal** — what they want to achieve, and what success looks like. *"What would a good quarter
  on [theme] look like to you?"*
- **Reality** — where things stand and what has been tried. *"What's been getting in the way of
  [thing they've been circling]?"*
- **Options** — what could be done, generated by them. *"What's one approach you haven't tried
  yet?"*
- **Will** — the specific commitment and its timing. *"What will you have done by our next
  1-on-1?"*

Spread the questions across the stages, and write each one about this person's actual situation.
A question that would work for any employee is not worth asking. The manager's job is to ask, not
to answer.

## Step 6 — Frame feedback with SBI, or leave it out

If there is a concrete observation worth raising, frame it as **Situation → Behavior → Impact**:
when and where; what was observed, described rather than labelled ("answered before others had
finished their update", not "was dominant"); and what followed for the team, the work, or them.
SBI works the same for good work, and most managers underuse it there.

Include this only when the record contains a real observation. Don't manufacture feedback because
the template has a slot for it.

## Output

Headings, in this order: **Where things stand** · **Progress on development goals** · **What to
close before the meeting** · **Open action items** · **What's worth discussing** · **Questions to
ask** · **Feedback to give (SBI)**

**Flag anything to be careful about at the very top** — a wellbeing signal, a retention risk, a
pattern that suggests they may leave. One line, plainly, and clear that it's a read from the
evidence, not a conclusion.

**Leave out any section you have nothing for.** Delete the heading — don't keep it above "none
recorded". The exception is **Where things stand**, which is always present; with no history at
all it says so in a line, and it may be the only section. That's an honest output.

Keep it to one screen, and end with one line saying which sources it drew on. Don't write the
meeting for the manager — they'll run it.

Nothing is saved anywhere unless the user asks. If they want the brief kept, save it where they
keep their own notes. Don't log it to Effy AI as an activity: activities are shared with their
participants, and a prep brief is the manager's private read.
